# Cross-Lingual Embedding Analysis

This stretch experiment evaluated how well `bert-base-multilingual-cased` represents English and Arabic climate texts in a shared embedding space. I selected 10 English texts and 10 Arabic texts from `climate_articles.csv`, balanced across policy, science, adaptation, and impact topics, then extracted multilingual BERT embeddings using mean pooling over the final hidden states. The final cosine similarity matrix was `20 x 20`, covering English-English, Arabic-Arabic, and English-Arabic comparisons. The within-language similarities were higher than the cross-language similarity: English-English mean similarity was `0.7224`, Arabic-Arabic mean similarity was `0.8056`, and English-Arabic mean similarity was `0.6072`. This shows that multilingual BERT still keeps texts from the same language closer together on average, but it also captures meaningful semantic alignment across English and Arabic. The strongest expected cross-lingual match was `EN8 ↔ AR7` about the Mafraq solar power plant with a similarity score of `0.7595`. Other strong same-topic pairs included `EN1 ↔ AR1` about the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (`0.7375`), `EN4 ↔ AR4` about NASA and 2023 being the warmest year (`0.7282`), and `EN5 ↔ AR5` about Greenland Ice Sheet loss (`0.7140`). Lower but still meaningful same-topic scores appeared for World Bank climate adaptation funding (`EN3 ↔ AR2 = 0.6803`), Dead Sea water loss (`EN9 ↔ AR9 = 0.6537`), and COP28 fossil fuel transition (`EN2 ↔ AR3 = 0.6504`). Overall, the model did not make Arabic and English texts as close as same-language texts, but it successfully ranked many same-topic cross-lingual pairs above unrelated pairs.

For bilingual NLP deployment in the MENA region, these results suggest that a single multilingual embedding model can be a practical starting point for Arabic-English search, retrieval, and classification. A bilingual climate search tool, for example, could likely connect an Arabic query about the Mafraq solar plant, the IPCC report, NASA climate findings, or Greenland Ice Sheet loss with relevant English documents because those cross-lingual pairs received relatively strong similarity scores. However, the lower English-Arabic mean similarity compared with the within-language averages shows that the model should not be trusted blindly in production. A real bilingual system should evaluate ranking quality using local Arabic-English examples, monitor whether same-topic documents consistently rank above unrelated documents, and consider fine-tuning or calibration on regional climate data if higher reliability is required. In short, multilingual BERT is strong enough to support an initial bilingual NLP system, but production use in MENA should still include careful evaluation before relying on it for high-stakes search, retrieval, or decision-support workflows.
